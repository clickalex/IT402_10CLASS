Topic 1: Methods of Communication

Meaning: Methods (modes) are the ways we exchange information, ideas and feelings. There are 3 main methods.

(a) Verbal communication — using WORDS (2 types)

TypeMeansExamplesAdvantagesDisadvantages
Oral / SpokenTalking + listeningFace-to-face talk, phone/video call, speech, meeting, classroom teachingFast, instant feedback, personal touch, doubts cleared at onceNo permanent record, forgotten easily, not good for long/complex data
WrittenReading + writingLetter, email, SMS, report, notice, books, WhatsApp messagePermanent proof/record, can re-read, formal, reaches farSlow, no instant feedback, needs literacy, tone can be misunderstood

(b) Non-verbal communication — WITHOUT words (body language)

Facial expressions (smile, frown), gestures (wave, thumbs-up), posture (straight = confident), eye contact, touch (handshake), tone/pitch/speed of voice (called paralanguage), appearance/dress. Experts say body + tone carry most of the message's impact — words alone are not enough. Example: saying "I'm fine" in an angry tone + crossed arms = receiver believes the body, not the words.

(c) Visual communication — using PICTURES/SIGNS

Charts, graphs, maps, traffic signals, 🚫 no-smoking sign, logos, photos, videos, emojis. Biggest plus: crosses language barriers — a red cross or arrow is understood everywhere. Example: a pie chart shows "60% passed" faster than a paragraph.

Choosing the right method

SituationBest methodWhy
Urgent doubt with teacher nearbyOralInstant reply
Leave application to principalWritten (email/letter)Formal record needed
Teaching upset friendFace-to-face (oral + non-verbal)Feelings need tone + expressions
Road directions for strangerVisual (map/sign)Language may differ
Monthly sales to managerWritten + Visual (report + chart)Record + quick understanding
Exam line: "There are 3 methods — verbal (oral + written), non-verbal and visual. The choice depends on urgency, distance, audience and whether a record is needed."

Topic 2: The Significance of Feedback in Communication Cycle

The communication cycle (7 steps — draw in exam)

Sender → Encoding → Message → Channel/Medium → Decoding → Receiver → Feedback

  • Encoding = sender converts thought into words/gestures. Decoding = receiver interprets it.
  • Example: Teacher explains photosynthesis (sender/encoding) → speaks in class (message/channel) → student understands (decoding) → student nods / asks doubt (feedback).

What is feedback?

The receiver's response that flows BACK to the sender. It completes the loop and proves the message was received + understood. Without feedback, communication is one-way (like shouting into a tunnel).

Types of feedback

TypeMeaningExample
PositivePraise/appreciation"Great presentation, well done!"
Negative / CriticalPoints out error"Slide 3 data is wrong."
ConstructiveError + solution (BEST type)"Slide 3 is wrong — recheck sales figures and use bigger font."
DescriptiveStates facts, no judging"You spoke for 12 minutes with 10 slides."
SpecificPinpoints exact point"Your introduction was clear." (vs vague "good job")
Immediate / DelayedInstant vs laterNod in class vs exam result after a month

Significance — why feedback matters (learn 6)

  1. Confirms understanding — sender knows message reached correctly.
  2. Corrects errors early — wrong data/impression fixed before damage.
  3. Completes the cycle — turns one-way talk into two-way communication.
  4. Improves performance — students/employees learn and grow.
  5. Builds trust & relations — honest feedback shows care and respect.
  6. Helps the sender improve — teacher adjusts speed, speaker improves style.

Qualities of GOOD feedback

Specific (exact point) • Timely (soon) • Descriptive (facts, not insults) • Constructive (suggest fix) • Polite/courteous • About the WORK, not the person ("the report needs dates" ❌ not "you are careless").

Topic 3: Measures to Overcome Barriers in Communication

The 7 barriers (with example each)

BarrierMeaningExample
1. PhysicalEnvironment disturbancesNoise in class, bad phone network, long distance
2. Linguistic / LanguageDifferent languages, jargon, tough wordsFrench manual for Hindi-speaking worker; doctor's Latin terms
3. CulturalDifferent customs/gestures/values👍 means "good" in India but is rude in some countries
4. EmotionalFeelings block listeningAngry student can't hear teacher's advice; stage fear
5. PerceptualDifferent viewpoints/assumptions"He is always late" — judging before listening
6. OrganisationalCompany structure blocks flowStrict hierarchy — worker can't reach manager; no suggestion box
7. InterpersonalPoor relations between peopleDistrust, interruptions, jealousy between teammates

Measures to overcome them (barrier → solution)

BarrierMeasures
PhysicalSilent room, working mic/network, right distance, proper seating/lighting
LinguisticSimple words, short sentences, common language, explain jargon, use translator/visuals
CulturalLearn other's customs/greetings, respect festivals, avoid offensive gestures
EmotionalStay calm, control anger, listen patiently, build confidence, right time to talk
PerceptualListen fully before judging, keep open mind, ask questions, paraphrase to confirm
OrganisationalOpen-door policy, suggestion boxes, clear channels, regular meetings, feedback system
InterpersonalBe polite, don't interrupt, respect others, build trust, active listening

8 golden general measures (for any 2/4-marker)

  1. Use simple, clear language — know your audience.
  2. Listen actively — eye contact, no interruption, paraphrase ("So you mean…?").
  3. Choose the right medium (urgent → call; record → email).
  4. Remove physical disturbances (silence phone, quiet room).
  5. Always give and ask for feedback.
  6. Use visuals (charts, demos) for tough ideas.
  7. Control emotions; be polite, patient and confident.
  8. Repeat/summarise key points at the end.

Topic 4: The Principles of Communication (The 7 Cs + more)

Good communication follows fixed principles. The most famous = 7 Cs:

#PrincipleMeaningExample
1ClearSimple words, one idea at a time✅ "Submit the file by Friday 5 pm" ❌ "Do it soon-ish"
2ConciseShort, no extra words✅ "Meeting at 10" ❌ "I wanted to tell you that there will be a meeting…"
3ConcreteSpecific facts/figures✅ "Sales rose 20%" ❌ "Sales rose a lot"
4CorrectRight grammar, facts, spellingCheck dates, names, numbers before sending
5CoherentLogical order, linked sentencesFirst → Next → Finally (not jumping randomly)
6CompleteAll needed info: what, when, where, whomInvite with date + time + venue + contact
7CourteousPolite, respectful"Please", "thank you", "sorry for the delay"

More principles (write any 2–3 as bonus)

  • Attention: listen/read fully; don't multitask while communicating.
  • Consistency: words match actions (don't say "be punctual" while coming late).
  • Timeliness: right info at the right time (exam date told early, not last night).
  • Feedback: always confirm understanding.
  • Proper channel: match medium to message (sensitive news face-to-face, not SMS).
Memory trick: "Can Cows Chew Cold Corn Cob Candy?" = Clear, Concise, Concrete, Correct, Coherent, Complete, Courteous.

Topic 5: Basic Writing Skills

Why writing matters

Permanent record • formal/professional • reaches many people far away • can be re-read and checked. Examples: applications, emails, reports, notices.

(a) The sentence — foundation of writing

A sentence needs Subject (who) + Predicate (what about them): "Amit (S) plays football (P)." Must start with CAPITAL letter, end with full stop ./ ? / ! 4 types: Declarative (statement — "I study IT."), Interrogative (question — "Where is the file?"), Imperative (order/request — "Open page 10." / "Please sit."), Exclamatory (feeling — "What a goal!").

(b) Parts of speech — quick table (NCERT favourite)

PartJobExample
NounName (person/place/thing)Amit, Delhi, book
PronounReplaces nounhe, she, it, they
VerbAction/staterun, is, write
AdjectiveDescribes nountall boy, red pen
AdverbDescribes verb/adj.runs fast, very tall
PrepositionShows relation (in/on/at)book on table
ConjunctionJoins words/sentencesand, but, because
InterjectionSudden feelingWow! Oh! Alas!

(c) Paragraph writing (3 parts)

  1. Topic sentence — main idea ("Yoga keeps students healthy.").
  2. Supporting sentences — 3–4 details/examples.
  3. Concluding sentence — ending line ("So every student should do yoga daily.").

(d) Articles + Phrases

Articles: A (consonant sound — a book) • An (vowel sound — an apple, an honest boy) • The (unique/specific — the Sun, the Taj Mahal). Phrase = group of words with NO complete meaning (no subject+verb), e.g. "in the morning", "on the table" — phrases BUILD sentences.

(e) Punctuation + Capitalization rules

  • Capital: sentence start, names (Amit), places (Delhi), days/months (Monday, June), "I", languages.
  • Full stop (.) ends statements • comma (,) separates items/pauses • (?) questions • (!) feelings • (') apostrophe for missing letters (don't) • (:) colon before lists.

Writing Do's and Don'ts

✅ Do❌ Don't
Plan points before writingStart without thinking
Short, clear sentencesLong confusing lines
One idea per paragraphMix many ideas together
Check grammar + spellingSend first draft
Polite formal wordsSlang (hey, yaar) in formal writing

1-markers (with answers):

  1. Name the 3 methods of communication. → Verbal, non-verbal, visual.
  2. Phone call is which type of verbal communication? → Oral.
  3. Tone of voice in non-verbal study is called? → Paralanguage (tone/pitch).
  4. Last step of the communication cycle? → Feedback.
  5. "French manual" barrier type? → Linguistic barrier.
  6. 👍 understood differently in countries = which barrier? → Cultural.
  7. Expand one C: message with facts/figures ("20%"). → Concrete.
  8. "Submit file by Friday 5 pm" shows which C? → Clear.
  9. "Wow!" is which part of speech? → Interjection.
  10. First line of a paragraph is called? → Topic sentence.

2-markers (model answers, 20–30 words):

  1. Explain oral and written communication with one advantage each. → Oral = spoken (phone, speech); instant feedback + fast. Written = letters/emails; permanent record + can be re-read.
  2. Why is feedback significant? (2 points) → (1) Confirms the message was understood correctly. (2) Corrects errors early and improves future performance.
  3. Suggest two measures to overcome language barriers. → (1) Use simple common words + short sentences, explain jargon. (2) Use visuals/translator and confirm with feedback.
  4. Explain any two Cs with examples. → (1) Clear — simple exact message ("Submit by Friday 5 pm"). (2) Courteous — polite words ("please", "thank you").
  5. Write two rules of good paragraph writing. → (1) Start with a topic sentence stating the main idea. (2) Add 3–4 supporting details + one concluding line; one idea per paragraph.

Deep Dive: Choose the Right Communication Method

Start with purpose, audience and a record

Communication is successful when the receiver understands the intended meaning, not merely when the sender finishes speaking. Before choosing a method, ask: What must the receiver know or do? Who is the receiver? How quickly is a response needed? Is a permanent record important?

  • Oral communication is useful for urgent clarification, discussion and immediate feedback. A telephone call can resolve a doubt quickly, but an important agreement should also be recorded in writing.
  • Written communication is useful for instructions, dates and information that must be checked later. Its limitations include delayed feedback and the risk that tone will be misunderstood.
  • Non-verbal communication includes gestures, posture, facial expressions and eye contact. It can support spoken words or contradict them. Do not assume that one gesture has the same meaning in every culture.
  • Visual communication uses pictures, signs, charts or diagrams. A labelled chart can explain a pattern faster than a long paragraph, but an unclear symbol can confuse the audience.

Worked example: announce a practical examination

A teacher needs to announce a practical examination and explain what students should bring. An oral announcement attracts attention and allows immediate questions. A written notice supplies the exact date, time, room and materials. A room map supports students who are unfamiliar with the building. Combining methods is often more effective than choosing only one.

Model answer: Use an oral announcement with a written notice. Speaking allows immediate clarification; the notice provides an accurate record that students can consult later.

Common mistake: Writing that oral communication is always better because it is faster. The best method depends on the purpose, audience and situation.

Deep Dive: Follow the Cycle and Improve Feedback

One message, seven stages

Imagine that a class monitor sends a message asking students to bring their notebooks tomorrow.

  1. Sender: The monitor initiates communication.
  2. Encoding: The idea becomes a sentence in a language the class understands.
  3. Message: “Please bring your IT notebook tomorrow” is the information being communicated.
  4. Channel: The message is sent through the class messaging group.
  5. Decoding: The classmate interprets what to bring and when.
  6. Receiver: The classmate is the person for whom the message is intended.
  7. Feedback: The classmate replies, “I will bring my IT notebook tomorrow.” This response lets the monitor check understanding.

Noise or a barrier can interfere at different stages. A weak connection may prevent delivery; an unfamiliar abbreviation may prevent correct decoding. A read receipt shows that a message was opened, not that its meaning was understood.

What makes feedback useful?

Useful feedback is specific, timely, respectful and connected to an action. It describes an observable result rather than attacking a person's character. Positive feedback identifies what worked; negative feedback identifies a problem. Constructive feedback helps the receiver improve by explaining the next step.

Weak feedbackMore useful feedbackWhy it helps
Good work.Your subject line clearly states the purpose of the email.Identifies the successful feature to repeat.
Your writing is bad.Add the meeting date and room number so everyone can attend.Focuses on missing information, not the person.
Speak properly.Slow down and pause after each main point.Gives a specific, achievable action.

Model answer: Feedback completes the communication cycle by showing how the receiver interpreted the message. It helps the sender correct misunderstandings and improve the next message.

Check yourself: Is silence proof of understanding? No. Ask a question or request a short explanation in the receiver's own words.

Deep Dive: Diagnose Barriers Before Choosing Solutions

Use the pattern: clue → barrier → remedy → reason

In a case-study question, identify evidence from the situation. Name the most relevant barrier, propose a practical remedy and explain why that remedy addresses the cause. More than one barrier may exist; do not list every barrier without linking it to the case.

Clue in the situationLikely barrierSuitable remedy
Construction noise makes instructions inaudible.Physical barrierMove to a quieter place and repeat the instructions.
A new student does not understand technical abbreviations.Linguistic barrierUse familiar words and explain necessary terms.
Anxiety makes a student unable to concentrate.Psychological barrierCreate a calm setting, listen and allow time to respond.
A gesture is interpreted differently by two groups.Cultural barrierExplain the intended meaning and avoid assumptions.
A receiver rejects an idea because of a fixed opinion.Perceptual barrierAsk questions, consider evidence and clarify viewpoints.
A learner cannot hear an oral announcement clearly.Physiological barrierProvide accessible written information and suitable support.
Instructions change while passing through several levels.Organisational barrierClarify responsibility and use a direct, documented channel.

Worked case: a rushed instruction

A supervisor says, “Submit the EOD report ASAP,” to a new trainee. The trainee is unsure what the abbreviations mean and is afraid to ask.

Analysis: Unfamiliar abbreviations create a linguistic barrier. Fear of asking creates a psychological barrier. Replace the instruction with, “Please send today's sales report by 5 p.m.” Then invite the trainee to explain the task back in their own words.

Common mistake: Saying only “communicate properly.” A remedy must be specific enough that someone can actually carry it out.

Deep Dive: Apply All Seven Cs to One Message

Repair an unclear message

Before: “Hey, send that thing soon. You never do it right.”

After: “Hello Riya, please email the revised science-project budget to the class teacher by 4 p.m. on Friday. Include the item names, quantities and total cost. Thank you.”

PrincipleWhat to checkHow the revised message applies it
ClearIs the main purpose easy to understand?It asks for the revised science-project budget.
ConciseAre unnecessary words removed?It gives only information needed for the task.
ConcreteAre details specific rather than vague?It states the deadline and required contents.
CorrectAre language and facts accurate?The sender must check the name, date and recipient before sending.
CoherentDo the ideas follow a logical order?Request, deadline and required details appear together.
CompleteCan the receiver act without guessing?The message identifies what to send, to whom and by when.
CourteousIs the tone respectful?It uses “please” and “thank you” instead of blame.

Distinctions often tested in MCQs

  • Clear versus concrete: Clear means understandable; concrete means specific. “Arrive soon” is easy to read but lacks a specific time.
  • Concise versus complete: Concise does not mean deleting essential details. A short message that omits the venue is incomplete.
  • Correct versus coherent: Correct concerns accurate language and facts; coherent concerns the logical connection between ideas.
  • Courteous versus positive feedback: Courtesy is respectful expression even when reporting a problem. It does not mean pretending every result is good.

Practice: Rewrite “Meeting tomorrow. Come prepared.” Add a purpose, time, venue and materials. Check your answer against all seven principles.

Deep Dive: Build Accurate Sentences and Paragraphs

Parts of speech in context

A word's role depends on how it is used. In “We drink water,” “water” is a noun. In “Water the plants,” “water” is a verb. Identify the job performed by the word in the sentence rather than memorising it as one fixed category.

  • Noun: names a person, place, thing or idea — “The student writes.”
  • Pronoun: replaces a noun — “She writes neatly.”
  • Verb: expresses an action or state — “They are ready.”
  • Adjective: describes a noun or pronoun — “This is a clear message.”
  • Adverb: modifies a verb, adjective or another adverb — “He speaks slowly.”
  • Preposition: shows a relationship — “The book is on the table.”
  • Conjunction: joins words or clauses — “Read the notice and bring your notebook.”
  • Interjection: expresses a sudden feeling — “Oh! I forgot my pen.”
  • Article: introduces a noun — “a book,” “an email,” “the classroom.” Follow the terminology used in your textbook.

Articles depend on sound

Use “a” before a consonant sound and “an” before a vowel sound: “a university” begins with a “y” sound; “an hour” begins with a vowel sound because the “h” is silent. Use “the” when referring to a specific item known to the reader.

Agreement, tense and punctuation

IncorrectCorrectExplanation
She write a letter.She writes a letter.A singular third-person subject takes “writes” in the simple present.
They is ready.They are ready.A plural subject requires the plural verb form.
Yesterday I go to school.Yesterday I went to school.A completed past action needs the past tense.
Its a useful book.It's a useful book.“It's” means “it is”; “its” indicates possession.
where is your notebookWhere is your notebook?A question begins with a capital letter and ends with a question mark.

Sentence purpose and sentence structure are different

A declarative sentence states something; an interrogative asks a question; an imperative gives a request or command; an exclamatory expresses strong emotion. These describe purpose. Simple, compound and complex describe structure.

  • Simple: “The student revised the lesson.” One independent clause.
  • Compound: “The student revised the lesson, and she attempted the quiz.” Two independent clauses joined together.
  • Complex: “The student attempted the quiz after she revised the lesson.” An independent clause plus a dependent clause.

Construct a paragraph in four steps

  1. Write a topic sentence stating the main idea.
  2. Add supporting details that explain the idea.
  3. Include a relevant example or reason.
  4. End with a sentence that brings the paragraph back to its main point.

Worked paragraph: Feedback improves classroom communication. It helps teachers discover whether students have understood an instruction. For example, asking a student to explain a task in their own words can reveal a missing step. Specific and respectful feedback therefore helps both teachers and students improve.

Editing checklist: Check the purpose, logical order, subject–verb agreement, tense, articles, spelling, capital letters and punctuation. Read the paragraph aloud to notice missing words or overlong sentences.

Exam Workshop: Explain, Apply and Self-check

Match the command word

  • Define: Give the meaning in one accurate sentence.
  • List or name: State the requested points without unrelated explanation.
  • Explain: State the idea and say how or why it matters.
  • Differentiate: Compare both items using the same basis, such as speed of feedback or permanent record.
  • Suggest or justify: Recommend an action and give a reason tied to the situation.

Worked two-point answer

Question: State two ways to overcome a linguistic barrier.

Model answer: First, use simple words that the receiver understands instead of unnecessary jargon. Second, explain unfamiliar terms and ask the receiver to repeat the instruction in their own words to check understanding.

Why it works: It gives two distinct actions. Each action addresses language or meaning, rather than an unrelated problem such as poor lighting. Follow the marks and instructions printed on the actual question paper; these notes are practice guidance, not a marking scheme.

A ten-minute recovery routine

  1. Spend two minutes reading the explanation of your weakest topic.
  2. Close the notes and spend two minutes recalling the definition and one example.
  3. Spend three minutes answering a relevant question without looking.
  4. Spend two minutes comparing your answer with the model and correcting omissions.
  5. Spend one minute writing what confused you and what you will do differently next time.

Final self-check: Can you select an appropriate method, trace feedback through the cycle, match a barrier to its remedy, improve a message with the seven Cs and edit a short paragraph? If not, return to the corresponding deep-dive section and try its worked example without looking.
